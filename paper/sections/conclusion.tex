\section{Limitations and conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
\paragraph{Limitations.} At about 1--1.35 tokens/s, \method{} is suitable for interactive use with short answers and for offline jobs, not for serving many users. It runs one sequence at a time, and long prompts take minutes (about 9 minutes for 16K tokens). The 2--3-bit experts lower agreement with the API on open-ended text to 86.0\% of tokens, although retrieval is not affected; machines with more disk can store lossless MXFP4 experts with a one-line change. All results come from one machine, with short prompts and short decode blocks; we did not measure decode at long context. We did not compare against other engines: the community engines we cite run related checkpoints with different quantization on other machines, and porting them to this setting was out of scope. A Mac with a faster SSD or more memory would shift the balance between the techniques we measured. Our screening study covers 16--32K keys and does not settle the question for contexts of 128K tokens and more.

\paragraph{Conclusion.} We asked what expert parallelism becomes on a laptop where the only other device is an SSD. Reading experts into fixed GPU buffers without copies replaces the token dispatch of a cluster, and an LRU cache replaces expert replication. A simple fit of the resulting chain describes decode speed from the bytes read per token, and traces show the SSD idle for a third of each step. The cluster's overlap techniques recover little of that time, and naive prefetching makes it worse because its guesses delay the reads a token is waiting for. Issuing the guesses only after those reads land, idle-window speculation, fills the idle time and makes decode 11--27\% faster. With these pieces, a 24\,GB MacBook Air runs the 754B-parameter DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash at 1.23 tokens/s with a cache that keeps 9\,GB free for macOS, with outputs close to DeepSeek's own deployment. We release the engine, the converter, the tests and the measurement scripts.
