\begin{abstract}
DeepSeek serves its 754B-parameter DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash model with expert parallelism: the 384 experts of each layer are spread over many GPUs, tokens are sent to the GPUs that hold their experts, and communication is overlapped with computation. We study what is left of this design on a single laptop with 24\,GB of memory. Our system, \method{}, keeps the dense weights (7.6\,GB) in memory, stores the experts on the internal SSD, and after routing reads each missing expert with one zero-copy read into a preallocated GPU buffer. Decoding one token then becomes a chain: the router decides which experts to read only after attention, and the next layer waits for those experts. A two-parameter model of this chain fits measured decode speed within 2\% across cache sizes. The chain explains why the cluster's overlap techniques help little here. Overlapping cached and missing experts gains 5\%, and naive next-layer prefetching makes decode 5--11\% slower. Using an interleaved ablation, we trace the loss to guesses that delay the reads a token is waiting for. We fix it by issuing guesses only after the current layer's reads have landed, a scheme we call idle-window speculation; inserting the guesses at the cold end of the cache adds a small gain that is within our confidence intervals. It makes decode 11--27\% faster at every cache size we tested and does not change the output; in three traced tokens per setting, the SSD's busy time per step rises from 62--65\% to 73--89\%. On a MacBook Air, \method{} decodes 1.23 tokens/s with an expert cache that keeps 9\,GB free for macOS (1.35 tokens/s with a larger cache). Its next-token predictions agree with DeepSeek's API on 86.0\% of open-ended continuation tokens (96.7\% averaged over 17 cases that include 12 single-token retrieval checks), and it retrieves correctly at every tested length up to 16K tokens. We also show that, when the scales of each FP4 block product differ by at most $2^9$, the sparse-attention indexer can use BF16 matrix units without changing its scores; with a fused kernel this makes an indexer query block about $2\times$ faster in long prefill ($1.8$--$2.1\times$ on three layers, $3.5\times$ on one).
\end{abstract}
